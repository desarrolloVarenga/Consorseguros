OECD report on 'Responding to the COVID-19 and pandemic protection gap in insurance'

OECD report on 'Responding to the COVID-19 and pandemic protection gap in insurance'

  • Francisco Espejo Gil
  • - Deputy Director for Studies and International Relations
  • - Consorcio de Compensación de Seguros
f X in

On 28 May 2020, within its series of publications and notes on policies to respond to the coronavirus (SARS-CoV-2) and the disease it produces (COVID-19), the Organisation for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD) released its note titled “Responding to the COVID-19 and pandemic protection gap in insurance”.

This is a timely note that gives context to all the issues currently on the table in the debate on how to respond from an insurance standpoint to this type of risk which had never previously materialized with such force in our lifetimes.

From the insurance point of view, the key problem in the present situation caused by COVID-19 is business interruption loss without physical damage, produced by the government adoption of preventive measures to contain the spread of the virus, with huge impacts on sectors such as commerce or hospitality that threaten the viability of thousands of companies, particularly small and medium-sized enterprises. In certain jurisdictions, this has put insurers under some degree of pressure from industry associations or even governments to cover this risk, which has exposed the fact that pandemics are either one of the typical exclusions in insurance policies or else a risk that can be insured separately, but which has very limited take-up. In some cases, this pressure is leading to legal action.

The industry justifies its position by claiming that risks cannot be covered ex post if they have not been included and rated ex ante. Retroactive coverage can seriously compromise the solvency of insurers given that, both on a global scale and in national markets, the potential cost of this business interruption loss substantially exceeds collected premiums in this line of insurance. Besides this economic reason, there is a legal one: if losses not agreed under contract are covered, these contracts are not being fulfilled and, therefore, legal uncertainty is incurred. Thus, national supervisors in the major markets concur in asking insurers not to cover these uncontracted losses.

The industry has indeed responded in many different ways to this crisis: from postponing or partly refunding premium payments for the duration of the lockdown period to setting up solidarity funds, occasionally on an extremely substantial scale, to assist those segments most affected by this crisis: health-workers, the hospitality sector, etc. In the note, several examples of this approach can be found, and in the “News” section of this issue of the magazine, one can learn about how the Consorcio de Compensación de Seguros is collaborating, from the Spanish insurance standpoint, to alleviate this situation.

What this crisis has triggered is a debate on how insurance might cover losses in future pandemics. In some jurisdictions (the United States, the UK, France, or the EU as a whole), consideration is beginning to be given to approaches similar to those taken for other risks with a high business interruption loss component, such as terrorism.

The key issues that these systems would have to solve would be:

  • Coverage for business interruption loss without physical damage (a problem that insurers or systems covering terrorism risk already face). To date, the insurance model has been focused more on morbidity and mortality risks rather than lost profit derived from the standstill of economic activities.
  • Potentially astronomical cost of losses, which would result in premiums being very unattractive to potential policyholders.
  • A impact at a possibly global level due to both the spread of the causative agent and the simultaneous adoption of government measures in many different jurisdictions, which would reduce the possibility of geographical diversification of the risk and dilute the potential for reinsurance to distribute and offset losses across territories, also increasing the cost of reinsurance.

There would also be several different possibilities to achieve broader coverage for this class of risk, which are not mutually exclusive. The first would be to incorporate some kind of automatic extension into business interruption policies for this risk. The OECD recognises that, in catastrophe risk coverage schemes, systems that, like the Spanish one, have this type of automatic extension achieve broader coverage levels than those covering these risks on an optional basis. The second would be to guarantee public sector participation in the form of a backstop to cover insured losses beyond a certain level expressed, for example, in terms of percentage points of GDP. This way, protection and a market for this risk would be generated and coverage would be guaranteed in the most extreme situations. And the third would be to require more ambitious business continuity plans, for both the administration and insured companies, allowing for better risk management. Within these plans would be measures to facilitate remote working or conditioning insurance coverage to a certain level of investment in public healthcare, which would allow for a more effective response to these threats.

The natural parallel to these types of potential solutions, as already noted, are the systems for covering catastrophe and/or terrorism risks, of which the OECD report makes a comparative review, naturally including the Spanish system. However, it is necessary to make an important point regarding the capacity that this type of insurance solution would require, which should be substantially greater than that of current systems for catastrophe insurance. The note reminds us that business interruption losses generated in US SMEs alone by COVID-19 may amount to as much as twice the cost of total losses caused by the East Japan tsunami in 2011.

From the insurance point of view, the key problem in the present situation caused by COVID-19 is business interruption loss without physical damage, produced by the government adoption of preventive measures to contain the spread of the virus, with huge impacts on sectors such as commerce or hospitality that threaten the viability of thousands of companies, particularly small and medium-sized enterprises.
