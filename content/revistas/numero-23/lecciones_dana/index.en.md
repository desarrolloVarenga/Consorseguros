Something changed in the Region of Valencia on 29 October 2024. This is a region accustomed to the arrival of autumn bringing torrential rains that cause flooding, but which usually do not disrupt daily life. That day was completely different. A DANA (the Spanish acronym for a cut-off low or closed upper-level low storm system) dropped over 770 liters per square meter in just 24 hours. For context: that is the volume of precipitation the province of Valencia receives in 19 months.

What happened in Valencia a year ago has very few comparable precedents in Spain. Other recent natural events—such as the Lorca (Murcia) earthquake in 2011, the volcanic eruption on La Palma in 2021, or the Biescas (Huesca) campsite flash flood in 1996—caused far lower personal and material losses. Not even the Bilbao floods in the summer of 1983, which until then had been the largest recent natural disaster, comes close. What took place in Valencia surpassed everything by far. From the very beginning, we in the insurance industry were well aware that we were witnessing the worst natural catastrophe in our country's recent history.

## The Three Pillars of the Insurance System
Spain features an insurance model based on public-private partnership that allows for the robust management of climate risks. This model rests on three well-defined pillars: private insurers, Agroseguro, and Consorcio de Compensación de Seguros (CCS).

Private insurers cover personal injury and property damage to insured assets caused by rain, winds under 120 km/h, hail, snow, and wildfires (whether natural in origin or man-made).

Second are agricultural risks, where Agroseguro comes into play. Agroseguro is an insurance pool in which 16 entities join forces to protect crops and livestock against all types of weather events, from droughts to floods. Public-private collaboration is present here in several ways: on the one hand, CCS acts as reinsurer and co-insurer for the Spanish agricultural insurance system; on the other hand, public administrations promote coverage among producers through subsidies for various crops—aid that can sometimes cover up to 50% of policy costs.

Meanwhile, CCS is the public entity tasked with indemnifying personal injury and property damage to insured parties caused by floods, winds over 120 km/h, storm surges, earthquakes, and volcanic eruptions. All these extreme weather and geological phenomena are grouped under the common label of "extraordinary risks."

CCS is funded by a surcharge applied to all property and casualty policies, as well as life and accident insurance policies issued in Spain. This guarantees that any insured party—from a homeowner to a large corporation—receives compensation following any of the events listed above. If you hold private insurance, you hold extraordinary risk coverage. No administrative declaration or governmental decision is required to receive compensation.

This system is built upon a deeply solidary principle: the mutualization of risk. Every policyholder in Spain contributes a small amount so that, when tragedy strikes, no one has to face it alone. Thanks to this, CCS possesses sufficient resources to handle massive catastrophes like the one in Valencia.

Furthermore, the system stands out because it treats all risks equally. That is, the surcharge paid by someone living in a flood-prone area, such as the Levante region, is identical to that paid by someone living in an unexposed zone. Variations in the surcharge amount depend solely on the insured sum of each policy, not on the risk location.

## A Protocol for a Coordinated Response
Under normal circumstances, CCS manages claims arising from extraordinary risks using its own available resources. With over 70 years of experience, it has handled major crises. But Valencia was different. The intensity, the speed, and, above all, the sheer volume of losses exceeded every forecast.

The essential goal in such situations is to urgently handle a massive influx of claims. The floods generated 250,000 compensation requests. Considering the annual average of the previous 10 years, this claim volume was equivalent to four years of work for CCS—and this is where the insurance sector stepped forward.

Faced with this challenge, an historic agreement was signed on 13 November 2024 between the Ministry of Economy, Trade, and Business and UNESPA. A Special Operating Procedure (POE for its Spanish initials) was established, under which 38 insurance companies—representing 98% of the insurance market in Valencia—provided their collaboration to CCS to process claims and help expedite compensation payments. In short, while CCS remained responsible for paying the claims, it delegated part of the processing for losses in the province of Valencia to private insurers. Meanwhile, CCS directly assumed management of damage in other territories such as Almería, Cuenca, and Castellón.

The result was an unprecedented mobilization. Over 8,000 professionals worked both on the ground and remotely, coordinating efforts and keeping the assistance network running non-stop. These included claims handlers, methodically managing each file with continuous oversight; loss adjusters, evaluating damage with precision; call center staff, taking note of policyholder requests and resolving urgent inquiries; and more than 1,000 insurance brokers, acting as a close and permanent bridge between policyholders and the system.

## A Technological and Procedural Challenge
The protocol provided the legal framework enabling collaboration between private insurers and CCS. But beneath this umbrella, an IT infrastructure and operational processes had to be created to allow four dozen insurance companies to coordinate with the institution.

In just 15 days, an IT platform capable of handling tens of thousands of claims had to be developed—with no time for preliminary testing. The tool was launched on the fly, using an existing IT solution for windstorm claims as its baseline. The system was tested in real-time and fine-tuned based on practical experience, inter-entity collaboration, and extreme dedication. Meetings took place daily and at all hours; many extended late into the night and across weekends. The situation demanded it, and the citizens of Valencia and other parts of southeastern Spain affected by the floods needed it.

The challenge was not only building technology gateways to transfer data between insurers and CCS. It was also necessary to adapt procedures and damage valuation criteria, given that private entities were acting on behalf of CCS.

## The DANA in Figures
The magnitude of the disaster is reflected in both the number of claims and the estimated economic impact. The 250,000 compensation requests generated by the DANA provide a clear picture of the scale of the problem facing residents of the Horta Sud district in Valencia, both personally and materially. The estimated cost, according to CCS data, reaches 4,800 million euros—a staggering figure that puts into context the extraordinary size of the event and the pressure exerted on the compensation system.

The breakdown of this figure speaks for itself and illustrates the broad scope of damage across all affected sectors:

- 82,000 claims corresponding to homes and homeowners' associations.
- 144,000 automobile claims.
- 21,000 compensation requests for shops, offices, and industrial facilities.
- 74 claims for damage to civil infrastructure.

Almost 50% of the claims received were processed by private insurance entities on behalf of CCS. Specifically, they managed 116,000 claims and facilitated the payout of 1,250 million euros in compensation. Insurers primarily handled mass-market lines: specifically, 58% of auto claims, 39.5% of home insurance claims, and 18.5% of commercial property claims passed through their hands.

This allowed CCS to focus on managing the most complex files, such as structural damage to residential buildings or business interruption in industrial plants. This joint effort reduced response times and delivered faster solutions to policyholders—a clear example of effective collaboration that made a difference and highlights the continuous evolution and improvement of the Spanish insurance system.

## Claims Assumed by Private Insurance
While the lion's share of the DANA fell upon the shoulders of CCS, private insurers also had to absorb a significant claims load resulting from the event. Outside the POE framework, they managed 138,454 claims caused by hail, rain, and winds under 120 km/h across all provinces affected by the DANA. These events entailed payouts exceeding an additional 180 million euros.

Meanwhile, Agroseguro covered agricultural damage, where persimmon and citrus crops—of major economic and social importance to the region—were hit particularly hard. To a lesser extent, though still with significant impact in certain counties, vegetable crops and vineyards were also damaged. In total, the phenomenon affected nearly 26,000 hectares of farmland spread across approximately 50,000 plots belonging to professional farmers and small family holdings. The total cost of indemnities paid out by Agroseguro reached around 60 million euros.

## Lessons Learned
So, what have we learned following the DANA? The floods served as a full-fledged stress test for Spain's public-private insurance model. Although there were hurdles and many decisions had to be made under extreme time pressure, the framework worked. We responded swiftly, implemented tools in record time, and built bridges between institutions. All of this was possible thanks to the solid foundation already in place, but also because we knew how to collaborate, communicate, and act with agility, composure, and immense dedication.

Now that we have seen what we are capable of achieving under pressure, we ask ourselves: why not prepare the system so that this collaboration triggers automatically, without the need to improvise?

The major lesson left by this experience is clear: we need predefined, shared, automatic operating protocols. Protocols that, upon crossing certain thresholds or criteria, activate without debate or delay to set a common mechanism in motion.

Imagine a specialized technical committee that, faced with a catastrophe, presses a button—and with that single action, immediately deploys a collaborative network encompassing insurers, public authorities, technical services, and digital platforms. This network would feature clear claim assignment criteria to eliminate errors or duplication while keeping policyholders informed. This last point is crucial: when immediate indemnity payment is impossible, a transparent system keeping the policyholder updated on file status in real-time is vital. Likewise, the more complex the processing, the more agile the advance payment system must be.

What we learned a year ago is that we can do it. Now, what we want is to improve. We want to transform what was an emergency response into an anticipatory model, featuring clear, agreed-upon rules activated instantly. We want to adapt our protection system to new realities.

## Closing the Protection Gap
The other major lesson from the DANA that we cannot ignore is that, despite the high insurance penetration rate in the province of Valencia (75% of homes were covered, as were 100% of vehicles), not everyone was protected.

Despite the collective effort and the solid response system, many people affected by the flooding lacked an insurance policy to help them rebuild their lives. As a result, after losing everything, several families could only rely on direct aid from central or regional government authorities.

Having insurance enables a return to normality. This was demonstrated barely six months prior to the DANA during the El Campanar building fire. An entire residential complex was consumed by flames, but thanks to insurance—both community policies and individual home coverage—reconstruction was able to begin.

Our challenge is to ensure that every home, business, and industrial facility in Spain is insured. This is not merely about protecting material goods; it is about protecting life projects.

Yet a total lack of insurance is not the only challenge to address. We must also ensure that protection is adequate—preventing underinsurance. This occurs either because policyholders lack sufficient coverages (consider, for instance, the vital importance of business interruption coverage for enterprises during events like the Valencia DANA) or because insured sums are inadequate. Being insured is not enough; one must be properly insured.

This requires us to promote a culture of responsible insurance and improve risk awareness. Insured amounts must reflect the actual value of the property, and policyholders must actively engage in conscious risk prevention.

### Conclusions
Extreme weather events are becoming increasingly frequent, intense, and widespread. We live in an era where meteorological risks have become a daily reality. This is our present context: unstable and vulnerable.

In this context, insurance plays a fundamental role: it provides stability to society as a whole, prevents hard-earned progress from being lost, and becomes especially vital in extreme situations.

Prevention, awareness, and public-private partnership are essential to anticipate risks, minimize the impact of natural phenomena, and accelerate recovery. If we have learned anything from the DANA, it is that in turbulent times, insurance is the only promise of continuity.
