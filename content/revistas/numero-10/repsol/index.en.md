Before broaching the subject we examine here, we must give our thanks for the kind invitation sent to IGREA (Spanish Association of Risk and Insurance Managers), of which Repsol is a member, to both the Consorcio de Compensación de Seguros and IGREA for having thought of Repsol in giving us the opportunity to portray, based on our experience and sphere of activity, the best outlook required to manage this type of risk in a changing environment.

Discussing environmental risks in a company like Repsol means delving right into one of the most important subjects for a company in the energy sector. Due to the inherent nature of their activities, oil companies are exposed to a whole raft of risks of the environmental kind that have to be managed effectively and properly. To do this, a preliminary effort must be made to identify these risks, which is what will enable preventive measures to be designed and implemented to avoid their occurrence and, where appropriate, to minimise their fallout if they occur. It is in this last case where the work of insurance managers takes on particular significance, since they are the people that have to find the most suitable mechanisms for covering these risks via the solutions offered by the insurance market.

The importance of environmental risk management is key to guaranteeing the long-term sustainability of a company. To illustrate how critical this is, we would cite as an example one of the most major environmental incidents in our industry, namely the spill which occurred in the Gulf of Mexico in 2010 during the drilling of the Macondo well. The consequences were disastrous: besides the unfortunate death of 11 people on board the platform, the latter was a total loss, estimated at over 400 million dollars, and this did incalculable damage to the reputation of the companies involved which, by extension, affected the entire oil & gas sector.

The environmental impact was equally high: some 5 million barrels of oil spilled into the sea, causing the death of over 800,000 sea birds and roughly 1,000 dolphins that were found washed up along the shores of the Gulf of Mexico. The pelican population in the zone was cut by 12 % and both fishermen and tourism companies suffered big losses. Although the figures are still provisional, in 2016 the operating company of the platform announced that the financial cost (including, among other things, cleaning and remedial costs, fines, damages for third parties, etc.) would be over 60 billion dollars before tax. The consequences of a catastrophic environmental incident for a company can be dramatic and can range from leading to its demise, as could happen to a medium-sized company such as Repsol, to inflicting financial damage that is extremely hard to recover from, even before considering reputational aspects, which would without a doubt be severely affected.

Although the example of Macondo may be considered extreme, companies such as Repsol are, to a greater or lesser degree, exposed to these risks. In Repsol’s case, the environmental risks it faces are essentially determined by the various activities pursued and the different places of operation. Repsol’s activity as a multinational energy company focuses on the entire hydrocarbon value chain: from extracting the raw material (oil and gas), known as Upstream or E&P business, through to manufacturing and marketing the whole range of derivative products, the Downstream business. Oil companies with both Upstream and Downstream activities are also referred to as integrated companies. With a production of 700,000 barrels of oil equivalent per day (of which over two thirds is gas) and refining capacity (converting crude oil into marketable products such as petrol, aviation kerosene, gasoil/diesel, etc.) of 1,000,000 barrels per day, Repsol can be said to be a medium-sized oil company compared to the larger oil companies, known as majors.

Repsol’s presence is global in the Upstream business (present in over 20 countries), while being more local on the Downstream side, where the focus is mainly on the Iberian Peninsula and Peru, although products are marketed in many other countries. After a material process of adjusting our portfolio over the past decade, culminating in the acquisition of the Canadian company Talisman Energy in 2015, Repsol has stepped up its Upstream side, boosting its presence in OECD countries while also expanding both its offshore activity (oil and gas extraction and production out at sea) and non-conventional operations (production using hydraulic fracturing or "fracking").

{{< figura src="/images/numero-10/repsol01.png" caption="Figure 2. Presence of Repsol around the world." source="REPSOL" >}}

In line with the forecasts set out in the strategic plan for the 2018-2020 period, Repsol has embarked on a very exciting journey, which will represent laying the first stone in a sweeping transformation of the company's profile, with the acquisition of part of the low-emission businesses (hydropower and gas-fired combined cycle plants) and the marketing division of Viesgo. Thanks to this acquisition, Repsol enters fully into the electricity business with a portfolio of 750,000 customers and a generating capacity of 2.9 GW, becoming the fifth largest electricity marketer in Spain. The medium-term objectives are ambitious: for 2025, our pledge to shareholders is to have increased the customer base to 2.5 million, achieving a market share of over 5 % in the gas and electricity retail business. As a result of this deal, Repsol has started the so-called energy transition. In the coming years, the company will become a multi-energy provider, with special emphasis on using cleaner forms of energy, in line with the commitments achieved in the Paris Agreement, which imply a 40 % reduction in CO2 intensity by 2040.

This variety of divisions and activities in constant evolution, combined with a presence in multiple countries with different environmental regulations and legislation, makes environmental risk management a complex and demanding activity, making it even more necessary (if possible) to ensure that the process of risk identification, prevention, mitigation, and transfer works as optimally and efficiently as possible.

The environmental risk management process begins with risk identification. According to its activities, Repsol must manage both environmental risks and environmental vectors, with the objective of minimising the impact that its operations may have on its surroundings. As environmental vectors, we will consider water, atmosphere, raw materials, or waste that are inevitably affected by the very development of the activity, without any extraordinary event needing to happen. By environmental risks, we refer to accidental and unforeseen scenarios that can cause damage to the environment. These are the relevant risks from the standpoint of the insurance market.

In Downstream activities, the main environmental risk lies in the potential occurrence of spills, which can pollute the soil and the phreatic layer in onshore operations and the sea at offshore terminals. The elements most susceptible to causing leakage of pollutants to the environment are piping (mainly if buried, given the greater maintenance difficulties they present), storage tank bottoms, pumps, and drains, and, at offshore terminals, loading and unloading arms for raw materials and products. The substances with the greatest impact on the environment in the event of a spill are crude oil and gasoil/diesel.

It is, however, in Upstream activities where the greatest environmental risk lies in terms of severity of consequences and economic impact. As mentioned earlier, the occurrence of a blowout (Macondo) is undoubtedly the worst potential scenario from a safety and environmental perspective that a company conducting hydrocarbon exploration and production activities can face. From an environmental standpoint, the economic impact would be heightened if the blowout were to occur in an area rich in biodiversity.

Regarding risk prevention, Repsol has a health, safety, and environment policy whose main objective is to pursue all activities while considering personal health, safety, and environmental protection as essential values. To this end, safety and environmental criteria are incorporated into the entire life cycle of activities, ensuring proactive risk management to prevent harm to people and assets and minimise impact on the surroundings. Repsol’s commitment to sustainability takes the form of a global sustainability plan structured around six core pillars: climate change, people, safe operations, environment, technological innovation and ethics, and transparency. Regarding the environment, Repsol is committed to being part of the solution to climate change by carrying out efficient management of natural resources, so that only those indispensable to generating the most efficient energy with the lowest possible impact are consumed, guaranteeing facility safety through appropriate risk management.

To minimise impact on environmental vectors, Repsol uses the circular economy as its main lever. The circular economy is a new production model whose goal is to produce goods and services while reducing consumption and wastage of resources and raw materials. The circular economy is part of a systemic change in production chains and consumption patterns beyond waste prevention and reduction that benefits the economy as well as the environment. In this regard, Repsol has established within its sustainability plan different areas of circularity, such as redefining raw materials, using CO2 to manufacture plastics, employing external waste streams to feed refining processes, process efficiency and innovation, maximising water reuse, minimising discharges, and enhancing energy efficiency; valorising waste as raw material, product, or energy in projects such as recovering sludge from the wastewater treatment plant in the petcoke unit, using potassium adipate as fertiliser, or developing more sustainable products such as 100% recyclable asphalts, among others.

However, while minimising impact on environmental vectors is important to meet the commitments made in the Paris Agreement, environmental risks represent the potential for the greatest impact on the company in terms of long-term sustainability due to potential financial and reputational consequences, and it is on these that we will focus from here on.

Environmental risk management cannot be understood in isolation and separately from safety risk management. Robust safety management minimises the likelihood and consequences of spills into the environment. To guarantee facility safety, it is essential to manage process safety properly through the application of suitable design principles and good engineering, maintenance, and operating practices, aiming to prevent incidents that can cause the release of hazardous substances. Process safety is based on four fundamental pillars:

* The company’s commitment to process safety. The company must ensure an appropriate process safety culture, compliance with standards, staff competence, and the involvement of both management and all workers.
* Understanding hazards and risks. Companies that understand hazards and risks can allocate generally limited resources better and with optimal efficiency. Knowledge management in process safety is therefore important to ensure that all hazards are identified and risks analyzed.
* Risk management, by properly operating and maintaining processes, managing process changes to ensure risk remains within tolerable limits, maintaining equipment integrity, ensuring quality of materials, fabrication, and repairs, and preparing response plans for any incidents that may occur.
* Learning from experience. Companies must learn from their own and others' mistakes and convert them into opportunities to improve process safety. To do this, they must have robust incident investigation and management systems, track key indicators, and conduct audits within a continuous improvement system.

As part of the risk management process, Repsol establishes a set of measures called “safety barriers,” designed to prevent a hazard from materialising and provide protection or mitigate consequences when the hazard becomes a reality. These barriers can rely on physical safeguards (design, automatic emergency systems, active and passive protection systems, etc.) or management safeguards (procedures, training, operational control, etc.). Although all barriers are important in risk management, strictly from an environmental risk perspective, those that help most to prevent materialisation are facility design, maintenance and inspections, combined with risk monitoring as well as inventory isolation systems.

Among the aspects to consider during the facility design phase is the physical environment where equipment will be installed. Factors such as ground permeability or proximity to sensitive areas should be considered in conceptual design so that equipment placement and protection selection minimise environmental risks and potential environmental impact. Another key element is selecting equipment and piping materials to ensure adequate corrosion resistance. Crudes processed contain substances, such as sulphur or naphthenic acids, which are susceptible to causing corrosion under facility operating pressure and temperature conditions. Proper design is therefore key to preventing spills.

Furthermore, climate conditions at facility locations—often close to marine environments or rainy zones—along with the passage of time, favor material deterioration. It is essential to have adequate maintenance and inspection plans for equipment and piping, applying state-of-the-art inspection techniques with appropriate frequency to determine the condition of all facilities and estimate remaining useful life, even for hard-to-access areas like buried piping or storage tank bottoms. In addition to maintenance and inspection plans, Repsol utilizes early warning systems monitoring process variables, including a piezometer network at all facilities for early spill detection before negative environmental effects occur.

If, despite these measures, an unexpected spill occurs, it is essential to have properly designed and maintained inventory isolation systems to minimise the amount of product reaching the environment. Typical inventory isolation systems include remote automatic isolation valves in process units and product storage areas; quick disconnect systems on vessel loading/unloading arms that disconnect from the ship during unexpected vessel movement due to waves or other causes, isolating product and preventing environmental release; blowout preventers (BOP) in drilling wells and safety valves in production wells, which close under overpressure to prevent hydrocarbon release to the surface.

As part of the risk management process, and within its commitment to continuous improvement, Repsol carries out both internal and external auditing processes. Regarding external processes, risk engineering visits conducted by insurance companies are key. These visits represent one of the mechanisms allowing Repsol to remain in touch with industry best practices and implement improvements in facilities and processes, thereby reducing the risk of an event with safety and environmental consequences, while generating transparency and collaborative relationships with insurers.

Additionally, in Spain, the government establishes controls regarding environmental risks through Integrated Environmental Authorisations (IEA). An IEA is an operating permit issued by regional governments to industries posing environmental risks, setting out environmental conditions for operating activities and facilities. The IEA determines measures to prevent or reduce impact on environmental vectors, establishing limits and controls that must be respected. It includes an environmental surveillance program involving continuous emission source monitoring, environmental impact control, and reporting anomalies to authorities. Furthermore, competent authorities perform annual inspections of industrial plants to verify compliance with IEA limits and controls; serious non-compliance can lead to activity suspension until appropriate measures are implemented.

Despite spill prevention and early detection mechanisms, industrial history includes numerous serious accidents with major environmental consequences. It is therefore important to have proper emergency response plans in place, which is also a legal requirement in many countries. For example, in Spain, European Directive Seveso III was transposed through Royal Decree 840/2015 approving control measures for inherent major accident risks involving dangerous substances. Royal Decree 1196/2003 approving basic civil protection guidelines for control and planning against major accident risks involving dangerous substances also applies. These decrees require companies to have internal emergency plans for potential accident scenarios, maintaining necessary human, material, and technical resources to respond. Additionally, local governments establish external emergency plans in conjunction with companies to protect the environment and nearby populations.

Repsol maintains emergency plans structured across organizational levels: local plant level, business unit level, and company-wide level. For emergency response, the company maintains its own firefighting and spill containment resources, along with trained personnel regularly exercised through drills. It also maintains mutual aid agreements with nearby companies facing similar risks and agreements with specialized firms in major fire suppression and large-scale marine spill containment.

Once risks are known and identified, one of the most important tasks for any business organization in the current social, geopolitical, and financial environment is managing them within acceptable parameters to help provide reasonable assurance regarding objective achievement.

While managing traditional operational or regulatory risks has been integrated into business management for years and carries significant weight in decision-making, newer operational risks like reputation or image are receiving heavy emphasis, with progress made in identification and protection given potential severe impacts on financial results.

Although environmental risk management is not a new corporate risk management activity, it has gained greater weight and relevance recently due to heightened societal awareness of environmental preservation, efficient natural resource management, and impact minimization. This is combined with growing concern over climate change and activities posing risks of accelerating such change.

Repsol shares this growing concern for proper environmental risk management and protection. As a major oil and gas player present across the value chain from exploration to marketing, it is especially aware of consuming minimal essential resources to generate efficient energy with minimal environmental impact.

Although Repsol maintains extensive safety measures across all operations, as an oil and gas company, it remains exposed to potential environmental damage caused by safety measure failures or human error during operations.

For this reason, Repsol has worked intensively for years on risk management to identify risks, determine reasonable retention levels, and transfer remaining risk to the insurance market. The goal is to transfer financial losses resulting from materialized events to the insurance market. Internally, Repsol conducts extensive work identifying environmental risks associated with its activities and potential liabilities, estimating economic impacts.

Risk identification and consequence evaluation are continually revised to utilize reliable, current information as the foundation of effective risk management. Operating across multiple countries, country-by-country and business-by-business risk identification is essential for designing a global insurance program as an efficient protection mechanism that yields significant cost savings.

Once environmental risks are identified and economic impacts evaluated, the next step is defining Repsol’s risk policy. Defining this policy determines the amounts the company itself will bear during a loss.

At Repsol, risk retention occurs primarily through its captive insurance company. This subsidiary insures part of Repsol's risks, aggregating asset portfolio risks to standardize coverage, generate economies of scale, significantly improve global risk management, and gain coverage flexibility in reinsurance markets.

Repsol reviews its risk policy annually, utilizing actuarial reports from external advisors to propose captive risk retention ranges based on expected losses. Captive risk retention helps Repsol secure better coverage terms from reinsurers by moderating transferred market risk.

Market risk transfer is conditioned by market capacity and availability. Key challenges Repsol encounters transferring environmental liability risk include lack of or costly offshore risk coverage, difficulty obtaining reasonably priced coverage for fracking activities, and general market capacity limitations where purchasing higher indemnity limits is impossible or exorbitantly priced. In addition, insurance market responses are sometimes not agile enough to offer required legal coverage products.

Focusing on insurance program mechanisms protecting against environmental risks, protection is structured through multiple avenues, recognizing that environmental risk can cause soil, water, or air pollution affecting various coverages.

First, protection is obtained through sudden and accidental pollution coverage in general civil liability policies. This coverage applies when pollution is caused by a sudden, specific, identifiable event occurring during the policy period linked to company operational processes. The sudden and accidental nature of the event triggers coverage, responding to most harmful consequences including third-party claims and legal defense costs.

To complement general liability coverage, Repsol maintains a dedicated environmental insurance policy. In addition to sudden and accidental pollution events, this policy covers gradual pollution events arising from company activities. To optimize coverage and costs, Repsol’s environmental liability policy is underwritten with terms and limits separate from general liability policies.

While both policies cover pollution events, they offer distinct benefits. Environmental liability policies cover prevention costs for immediate environmental damage threats and avoidance costs when damage has materialized. They specifically cover costs for repairing strictly environmental damage, such as restoring affected flora and fauna.

A major feature of environmental insurance is coverage (though limited) for catastrophe management during environmental events, reflecting the close link between risk management and public perception.

In compliance with Environmental Liability Law 26/2007, Repsol recently subscribed to an insurance policy as a financial guarantee covering environmental liability inherent to its operator activities.

Third, environmental risk protection is provided through Exploration & Production (E&P) and Property Damage insurance via "Extra Expense of Operator" coverage. This covers cleanup and containment expenses associated with E&P activities or property damage events at Repsol assets.

This coverage activates only when covered risks materialize. For example, under E&P policies, if a well blowout causes ground pollution, coverage pays accidental pollution cleanup costs. Like general liability policies, environmental policies feature distinct terms and limits from E&P and Property policies to optimize coverage and costs.

While Repsol’s risk policy aims to secure maximum market coverage against catastrophic losses, market capacity remains insufficient to cover costs associated with major environmental incidents. This highlights the essential importance of prevention and mitigation initiatives.

In conclusion, energy is essential for economic development and improving living standards. Energy availability must align with efficiency, environmental commitment, sustainability, and accessibility. Risk management in energy companies is a central priority and necessary condition for sustainable progress. Repsol’s vision is to be a global energy company creating sustainable value through innovation, efficiency, and respect.

Heightening awareness involves everyone, but companies must promote preventive management to minimise unfortunate event consequences. At Repsol, all Management Committees begin with safety and environmental reporting as a fundamental premise.

Identifying, standardising, aggregating, and measuring risk are essential for proper management. Within Repsol’s risk map, there is zero appetite for environmental risks, prompting multidisciplinary scenario analyses for preventive management.

Repsol has facilitated insurer risk engineering inspections for over thirty years, leading the sector by implementing recommendations across company assets.

To mitigate materialized risks, Repsol’s insurance policy focuses on expanding coverage limits, identifying emerging risks, and stimulating opportunities across the insurance value chain.
