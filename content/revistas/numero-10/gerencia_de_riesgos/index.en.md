## 1. Introduction
In the dawn of applying risk management technique, the main aim was to protect the profit and loss account of companies by using and developing an assortment of applicable techniques involving identifying, assessing, eliminating, mitigating or ultimately transferring risks to specialist third parties and calling on the latter option as a suitable supplement when the previous measures referred to were ineffective or not possible in their entirety[cite: 13].

Fortunately, with the passing of time, we have witnessed the appearance of huge advances generally and, with this, similarly spectacular developments in the area of risk management, featuring the identification of emerging risks, new tools becoming available and the application of new concepts with broader aims than those mentioned earlier[cite: 13]. This is why the definition of risk management keeps on growing and now we find ourselves faced with situations that seek to protect not only the profit and loss accounts of companies, but also other capital elements such as assets, which are the core of activities; employees, which are the key engine of the business; suppliers, who are key partners in the process; the social and economic environment in the geographies in which operations take place; environmental awareness; corporate social responsibility; business reputation and ethics, and a whole raft of issues of a markedly strategic nature, such as an emphasis on nurturing talent and excellence, knowledge transfer, attracting and managing to retain crucial employees, sustainability, recycling, the circular economy, etc[cite: 13].

This said, and running a basic analysis, we start from the assumption that our business activities have their geographical origins in Europe and can extend at some later date to any corner of the world, which initially and fortunately provides us with a regulated environment (to a greater or a lesser degree) which leads to legal security in initiating business transactions[cite: 13]. This, together with the new protective techniques being developed in the field of risk management, allows us the precise guarantees with which to take on substantial dealings and ambitious business projects that are properly managed with the objective of achieving steady progress that lets us create wealth and accompany the development that society demands, with the host of changes and advances taking place that will go on occurring within a relatively brief time-span according to all forecasts[cite: 13].

## 2. The European Union Environmental Directive of 2004 (2004/35/EC)

{{< figura src="/images/numero-10/AGERS_CCS01.jpg" caption="" source="" >}}

After this short introduction, we now go on to delve into the matter at hand (risk management and environmental liability), using a chronological format as follows so as to gain a better understanding[cite: 13].

We can claim that article 45 of the Spanish Constitution of 1978 features as one of the most innovative and avant-garde aspects of the era in which it was published, while it remains fully valid to this day when its text says[cite: 13]:

{{< callout >}}
*“1. Everybody has the right to enjoy an environment that is suitable for their development, as well as the duty to protect it.*
*2. The public authorities shall ensure the rational utilisation of all natural resources to protect and improve the quality of life and both defend and restore the environment, while relying on indispensable collective solidarity.*
*3. Criminal or, as appropriate, administrative penalties shall be established on the terms determined in law for anybody who violates the provisions of the previous section, as well as the obligation to remedy the harm done”.*
{{< /callout >}}

There was thus a new and modern concern for the environment in Spain’s constitution that gave rise to the subsequent development of several far-reaching and varied laws in relation to this area (Regulations on Hazardous Waste, for example, and other legislation with similar content and ends in connection with the environment as mentioned)[cite: 13].

Nonetheless, afterwards, a second major episode took place which affects Spanish legislation regarding this matter, as a European Directive (2004/35/EC) was drawn up whereunder the repair of harm caused to the environment was transposed to Spanish law via the enactment of Law 26/2007 of 23 October on Environmental Liability, which, in its statement of legislative intent, establishes an administrative framework of strict and unlimited liability for the activities named in Appendix III thereof with respect to returning damaged resources to their original state, a framework based on the principles of the prevention and remedy of environmental damage that are defined in the expression the polluter pays[cite: 13].

{{< callout >}}
As it says in the preamble to the law in chapter I, “article 45 of the Constitution recognises the right of citizens to enjoy a suitable environment as an indispensable condition for the person’s development, while it establishes that those who fail to fulfil the obligation to use natural resources rationally and to protect nature shall be obliged to repair the damage caused irrespective of any administrative or criminal penalties that are also applicable.

This precept has been implemented by means of several different legal regulations which, although they have been expanded on and updated, have failed to prevent the repeated occurrence of accidents of a diverse nature that have had extremely grave consequences for the natural environment. This highlights the need to have environmental legislation that implements new systems of liability that effectively prevent environmental damage and, for those cases where this continues to happen, which ensure swift and appropriate repair”.
{{< /callout >}}

This need was addressed by Directive 2004/35/EC of the European Parliament and of the Council of 21 April 2004 on environmental liability with regard to the prevention and remedying of environmental damage, which was later transposed to the Spanish legal system[cite: 13]. It should be pointed out that this is an administrative regime, which includes a set of powers whereby the public administration ensures that the law is adhered to[cite: 13]. Thus a separation is made here between the liability referred to (administrative) and the classic concept of civil responsibility, which is assigned to the law courts when there are disputes between the parties concerned[cite: 13].

We can therefore say that the Spanish law of 2007 implements the original article 45 of the Spanish Constitution via the transposition of European Directive 2004/35/EC on environmental liability and guarantees that damage to the environment is remedied by the offending party, thereby avoiding the State or the Regional Autonomies having to bear the costs of the loss event[cite: 13].

As the preamble to the law and the European Directive itself mention, the preventive and remedial aspect in relation to environmental damage caused becomes fundamental, while these issues are very important for risk managers, since the concepts cited are vital and a cornerstone of our profession[cite: 13]:

{{< callout >}}
“The remedial side in the new system of environmental liability must not, under any circumstances, underestimate its preventive aspect. Instead, on the contrary, it must be given special attention, in terms of both its regulation and in its administrative application, as there is no better conservation policy than that of prevention with respect to environmental damage. This view justifies the universal application which the law confers to obligations in the matter of preventing and avoiding harm to the environment, by making adoption thereof extend to all manner of activities and in relation to all forms of behaviour, whether intentional or negligent, or merely accidental or unpredictable”.
{{< /callout >}}

Even so, from a critical and constructive standpoint, it is not necessary to conduct profound studies of the matter to conclude that the harmonisation of legislation in the European Union still requires large amounts of additional work and effort to achieve the ends that it pursues, while it can be confirmed that reaching these goals is also part of risk management[cite: 13]. It has clearly been found that there are still disparities in applying and interpreting the law, and on occasions situations arise where member states regulate however they like (note, for example and as regards tax matters, the tax on insurance premiums known as IPS in Spain) and the rates can vary, from countries where no tax applies, such as the Czech Republic, Slovakia, Hungary, Cyprus, Romania and Poland, to others where there are high rates for this, such as in Italy, where this stands at 22.25 %[cite: 13]. Further examples, for the sake of a better understanding, would be: Netherlands 21%, Belgium 9.25 %, Germany 19 %, Austria 11 %, France 9 %, Luxembourg 4 %, Spain 6 %, Ukraine 3 %, etc[cite: 13].

The same occurs with the regulation on captive companies (countries such as Luxembourg or Ireland, for example, with diverse legislation in this area compared to the other member states, where this question still has not been legislated for)[cite: 13].

Nevertheless, the preceding comments are only a taster before we move onto the core raison d'être for this article, which is based on the importance of environmental liability from the perspective of risk management[cite: 13]. It is a fact, and certainly praiseworthy, that, in the field of environmental liability, the source whence the law concerning this emanates can be found in the 2004 Directive in the guise of an issue that was of concern to lawmakers following the occurrence of certain major loss events (1976 – Seveso, Italy; 1978 – Lekkerkerk, Netherlands; 1986 – Basel, Switzerland; 1989 – Exxon Valdez, Alaska/USA; 1998 – Aznalcóllar, Spain; 1999 – Erika, France/United Kingdom, etc.) and which represented the beginnings of a wonderful modern and appropriate future vision advanced with a sense of sustainability to strike a balance among business activities, development, risk management and, ultimately, awareness about better conditions for future generations, thereby heading off these kinds of situations as far as possible[cite: 13]. Nonetheless, the Directive has been transposed with substantial timing differences among the member states and with variations as regards content (more lax application in certain cases or else different in the main and rigid in others, depending on the specific legislative conditions in each state and the degree to which these are implemented)[cite: 13]. The EC regulation established that it should be obligatory to effect transposition to the local legislation in all the member states by a deadline of 30 April 2007, yet this objective was not achieved until July 2010[cite: 13]. The following would represent some examples: Italy in 2006; Spain, Hungary and Slovakia in 2007; Bulgaria, Ireland, Malta, the Czech Republic and Portugal in 2008; France, Greece and Luxembourg in 2009, etc[cite: 13].

On the other hand, as we said, the Directive establishes a framework based on the polluter pays principle, aimed at preventing and remedying environmental damage[cite: 13]. This principle is already enshrined in the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union (Article 191 (2) TFEU)[cite: 13]. With the passing of time, other very significant loss events that have recently occurred outside the orbit of the European Union have confirmed the need to go further in such regulation, such as what happened in the Mount Polley mine in British Columbia, in Canada, in August 2014 or the bursting of the dam in November 2015 in Brazil[cite: 13].

In this respect, within the context of Spanish legislation, environmental damage is defined as that which endangers the protection of species and natural habitats, including harm to water and soil, as well as operators who pursue the hazardous activities that are stated in Appendix III of the Directive and who have strict liability (irrespective of whether fault exists)[cite: 13]. Other operators that are not included in Appendix III can be liable if fault exists based on detriment to the protection of species or natural habitats such that there is a causal nexus between the activity and the damage produced, it being underlined that, even if this affects natural or legal persons, non governmental organisations of the environmental type are entitled to apply to the competent authorities for appropriate remedial action to be carried out if these are held to be necessary[cite: 13].

The Directive is a living text that has been subjected to three amendments to adapt it to current needs that have been manifested in Directive 2006/21/EC on the management of waste from extractive industries, Directive 2009/31/EC on the geological storage of carbon dioxide, which has likewise amended other directives, and in Directive 2013/30/EU on safety of offshore oil and gas operations, also amending Directive 2004/35/EC[cite: 13].

It is important to mention that after verifying this situation over one year of work, the European Parliament issued the Resolution of 26 October 2017 on application of the Environmental Liability Directive, that included 51 points and interesting conclusions that can be consulted by readers[cite: 13]. In the section on the state of implementation (articles 3 to 7), we can see how a lack of clarity and consistency has been detected regarding key concepts, with a high degree of variability among the member states, and it was considered necessary to make additional efforts to standardise and interpret them in a similar way[cite: 13]. Here, seven states still have to resolve regulatory matters raised with regard to their transposition process, and the conclusion is that national legislative arrangements are still playing a substantial role that ought to have already been taken up by the Directive after all the time that has passed and the decisions taken[cite: 13]. This matter having been identified, articles 24 to 51 of the Resolution cited put forward significant suggestions as to how to enhance the harmonisation of the Directive[cite: 13].

On the other hand, one should also underline the large number of questions to be sorted out that have both arisen in this process and engendered a whole host of working groups and commissions to resolve them in the European Union[cite: 13].

One of these is the so-called ELD Multi-Annual Work Programme (MAWP), which was set up for the 2017-2020 period to adjust the Environmental Liability Directive itself and which has been developed in response to the assessment carried out by REFIT (European Commission's Regulatory Fitness and Performance Programme)[cite: 13]. This evaluation programme seeks to pick out the opportunities for simplification and cutting unnecessary costs whenever the Commission proposes that a pre-existing law be revised[cite: 13]. The initiatives that result in these efforts are included in the Commission’s work programme every year and are monitored by the body known as the REFIT Scoreboard[cite: 13]. In this case, clear gaps and loopholes, as well as shortcomings have been noted that require greater in-depth study and analysis to be addressed[cite: 13]. With this aim in mind, the intention is for MAWP 2017-2020 to be revised and brought up-to-date on an annual basis, while making any necessary changes, gaining a better understanding and picking up on new requirements, with the ultimate goal of achieving a better quality Environmental Liability Directive in keeping with its original objectives of preventing and remedying environmental damage caused based on the polluter pays principle and, additionally, helping to maintain the environment and protect the natural resources in the European Union (biodiversity, water and soil)[cite: 13].

The current MAWP has three key pillars for the period referred to (2017-2020)[cite: 13]:

1. Improving the evidence base for the evaluation process and decision-making on the part of the Commission, member states, stakeholders and practitioners (devising an assessment framework and a registry for the Directive mentioned)[cite: 13].
2. Supporting its implementation through tools and measures towards providing greater support in this respect (general and common understanding of terms and concepts, and building capacity applied to practical issues)[cite: 13].
3. Ensuring sufficient availability of financial capacity, in particular for large losses or in cases of insolvency (secure sufficient and available instruments to cover the liabilities indicated in the Directive)[cite: 13].

Having said all this, we consider that the European aspect has now been pointed out, so we shall go on to discuss the situation in Spain[cite: 13].

## 3. The Spanish Environmental Law of 2007 and application of Directive 2004/35/EC
As any decent risk manager should know, Law 26/2007 of 23 October on Environmental Liability establishes an administrative regime of strict and unlimited environmental liability for the activities contained in Appendix III, which is based on the principles of prevention and remedy of environmental damage (the polluter pays)[cite: 13]. The aforementioned law comprises 49 articles that are grouped into six chapters and a final part that consists of 14 additional provisions, one temporary and six definitive, as well as six appendices[cite: 13]. It is partly implemented by Royal Decree 2090/2008, in which a new system is established for remedying environmental damage whereby operators who harm natural resources or might cause such damage must take the necessary measures to prevent them or, if the damage has already been done, limit or impede the effects thereof, as well as return the natural resources to their former state before the harm occurred[cite: 13].

{{< figura src="/images/numero-10/AGERS_Aznalcollar02.jpg" caption="" source="" >}}

It is important to point out that not all natural resources are protected by this law[cite: 13]. For these purposes, only those which are protected under the so-called “environmental concept” are considered, such as damage to waters, soil, seashores, coastal inlets and species of wild flora and fauna that are permanently or temporarily present in Spain, as well as the habitats of all indigenous wild species[cite: 13]. This means that harm to the air or persons and their property are therefore excluded[cite: 13]. On the other hand, not all harm suffered by these natural resources will give rise to environmental liability[cite: 13]. For the law to be applied, there must be a threat of damage, or damage which has occurred, that produces adverse effects for the natural resource itself[cite: 13]. As regards soil, the concept also includes a clear risk of giving rise to negative effects on human health[cite: 13].

Aside from what we have mentioned, there are some peculiar features, some of which are very interesting due to the originality of the concepts they harbour and due to the decision by the Environment Ministry of the day, when Ms. Cristina Narbona held this ministerial portfolio, whereby the Directive was used as a rule of minimums, which was later given specific implementation in Spain according to the circumstances, experiences, strategies and needs in our specific setting[cite: 13].

We thus conclude by summarising that the aim of the Law is to transpose Directive 2004/35/EC of the European Parliament and of the Council on environmental liability with regard to the prevention and remedying of environmental damage into Spanish law[cite: 13]. The intention is for the polluter to be accountable for the damage caused to certain natural resources (soil, waters, wild species and protected habitats, seashores and rivers), which do not include harm to the air and persons or their properties, which are not covered by this Law, and focussing on pure ecological damage, such as to environmental natural resources, without taking account of whether these are publicly or privately owned[cite: 13]. The offending party is obliged to restore the resource to its original state or, should this not prove possible, make up for the damage by means of other actions in other places, and paying damages in monetary form to third persons is not allowed[cite: 13].

## 4. The financial guarantees in environmental law in Spain
The Spanish Environmental Liability Law of 2007 puts into effect two key principles in EU legislation with respect to the environment: the prevention principle and the polluter pays principle[cite: 13]. It therefore establishes a dual liability system: strict, for the activities in Appendix III, and fault-based liability, for other minor cases outside the appendix cited[cite: 13].

The competent administrative bodies for overseeing compliance with this law are the regional governments, except where the damage occurs in the public domain owned by the State, the public shoreline domain or in waters in the public domain, in which case the General State Government will take charge of such matters, guaranteeing consistency of judgement[cite: 13]. It does not have retroactive legal effects and is compatible with the requirement of other forms of liability, be these criminal or administrative[cite: 13]. Civil action by private individuals that are affected is also conserved, although, in article 5, the law prohibits double cost recovery[cite: 13]. This was enacted through Royal Decree 2090/2008 of 22 December endorsing the partial implementation regulations for Law 26/2007 of 23 October on Environmental Liability[cite: 13].

Having mentioned the foregoing, we now move on to discussion of the current state of legislation as regards Spanish mandatory financial guarantees[cite: 13]. Specifically, in chronological order, those which concern the activities in Appendix III, which are the first that have to be compliant in this regard and which lead us to the need to apply one of the tools which the law proposes in this sense (insurance policies, bank guarantees or ad hoc reserves) for loss events where the offending party is required to have the financial resources to address their economic consequences[cite: 13].

As is common knowledge, on 30/10/17, the Ministerial Order was published which contained the deadlines decided for the coming into effect of the obligation to set up financial guarantees to cover environmental liability in certain activities[cite: 13]. The original law of 2007 estimated that these would be issued “from 30 April 2010”, which means that there has been a long delay in publishing these[cite: 13]. This having been accepted, the 2016-2019 period was set as the target for regulating this matter[cite: 13]. Under Ministerial Order ARM/1783/2011 of 22 June, consideration was given to the priority and time-table for approving the ministerial orders based on which the setting up of the mandatory financial guarantee would be required[cite: 13].

With this episode having been overcome, the new Ministerial Order referred to concerns the owners of Level 1 priority activities (those which are subject to SEVESO rules and regulations, major combustion and recovery facilities, and the processing of hazardous waste), the financial guarantees for which have to have been duly set up as of 30/10/2018[cite: 13]. The owners of level 2 activities (a large portion of activities which are subject to IPPC rules and those which involve industrial emissions that are not classified as level 1) have a deadline for addressing this matter of 30/10/2019[cite: 13].

Here, the discipline of Risk Management plays an important role and an array of different actions have to be set in train at these departments at companies, such as, in some cases, confirming, and in other cases, finding out, the following information[cite: 13]:

1. An Environmental Risk Assessment (ERA), as required under the law[cite: 13]. Risk Management must take part in this process as a key element with respect to the risks to identify and the measures and decisions to take in order to comply with the preventive role which the law intends[cite: 13].
2. The results thrown up by this analysis in economic terms have to be found out by establishing the techniques applied as the basis for calculating these, the specific and readily available computerised tools to use for this purpose (MORA —Environmental Liability Supply Model—, etc.), and the criteria applied and the breakdown for these[cite: 13].
3. The figure resulting from the ERA must be at least the established legal minimum, including costs of prevention, and both complementary and compensatory remedy, as well as a possible safety margin as a previously made recommendation[cite: 13].
4. There must be explicit reference to the financial guarantee to be used[cite: 13]. Should it have been decided to take out an insurance policy for this situation, as we understand happens in most cases given that it is the easiest way to transfer risk to a third party, this option must be confirmed or an assessment made of the other alternatives that the law recommends (bank guarantee or an ad hoc underwriting reserve)[cite: 13]. It could be that the decision is made to use one of these options jointly, bearing in mind that, in spite of the calculations made, the liability is always limitless and the final figure might be greater than that determined in the risk assessment[cite: 13].
5. The Liability Statement featuring the minimum content required in Appendix IV of the Regulations must be filed with the competent administrative body[cite: 13].

These issues are key and, if it is decided to use insurance as the financial guarantee, we will have to follow the appropriate processing, obtaining insurance certificates with the right content that are issued in Spanish in the case of multinationals (as these will be processed through a Spanish administrative body)[cite: 13]. It is recommended that these include a suitable figure that is high than that resulting from the assessment made as a safety margin, bearing in mind the limited liability which the law mentions, and that they clearly show an additional clause confirming the existence of an automatic replacement rate so that, if a loss event occurs, the initial amounts in it remain stable to cater for any other possible loss events that might happen (either to a single company or a group of them all covered under the same policy)[cite: 13].

The point should be made that, within the scope of what the Directive permits, the financial guarantees mentioned were not initially obligatory and only eight states (namely Bulgaria, the Czech Republic, Greece, Hungary, Portugal, Slovakia, Romania and Spain) have decided to implement them in the transposition of the Directive into their national legal systems, whereas others have not included them (notable examples being: Germany, France and Italy)[cite: 13]. Finally, just to be make the circumstances even more confused, in certain countries, the communities or regions have also gone on step ahead in this direction and legislated according to what they have been allowed to do owing to transfers of jurisdiction, without any standardised criterion in favour of implementation in common, which leads us to the conclusions in the studies and analyses carried out by the European Union which reveal an outcome of a clear lack of consistency that is far from delivering the harmonisation desired as regards this matter[cite: 13].

With respect to this, the European Commission has issued reports on the effectiveness of the Directive and the availability of financial guarantees to cover the liability mentioned, and it has observed that there is a dearth of practical experience in implementing the Directive and in terms of its usefulness as regards remedying environmental damage[cite: 13]. With the goal of sorting out this issue, the European Commission has set in train specific actions, some already established in previous years, such as the staging of the working seminar by the name of Stakeholder and Practitioner on the Implementation of the ELD in November 2011[cite: 13]. The actions envisaged in this case were[cite: 13]:

1. Issuing an explanatory leaflet on the Directive[cite: 13].
2. Providing the member states with material on the Directive for training campaigns[cite: 13].
3. Exploring and exploiting links between the Directive and other legal provisions, such as the Directive on Habitats and the Framework Water Directive[cite: 13].
4. Evaluating additional aspects relating to risk assessment and determining the risk levels of industry in the European Union or to activities involving greater risk[cite: 13].
5. Conducting a comprehensive study of the viability of setting up a fund or similar instruments to tackle financial security/guarantees of European companies and industries within the context of the Directive[cite: 13].

This issue took on particular importance following the accident which occurred in Hungary on 4 October 2010 at the aluminium manufacturing enterprise Hungarian Aluminium Production & Trade Company – (MAL AG to use its Hungarian acronym)[cite: 13]. When a chemical disaster took place which featured toxic spillage on account of the bursting of one of the waste pools, it was discovered that the company had an insurance policy with insufficient cover according to the liability laid down by the Directive[cite: 13]. The loss event sparked a rethink in the European Union and among its member states in favour of obliging their industries to obtain financial guarantees with cover to match environmental risks and liabilities and using the necessary technical resources, while considering the potential effects of this should a loss event occur and establishing preventive measures and ultimately obtaining the financial guarantee most suited to requirements[cite: 13].

Nonetheless, on 30 October 2017, the BOE (Official State Gazette) published the Ministerial Order APM/1040/2017 of 23 October establishing the date required to meet the deadline for setting up the mandatory financial guarantee for those activities in Appendix III of Law 26/2007 of 23 October on Environmental Liability (classified as having priority levels 1 and 2 via Ministerial Order ARM/1783/2011 of 22 June amending the appendix thereof)[cite: 13]. In the case of Spain, the activities in Appendix III are classified according to the risks they entail as high, medium and low, while three groups of priorities are established which we might summarise by simplifying details as follows[cite: 13]:

* **Group 1 priority**. Facilities included in the SEVESO Directive (2003/105/EC) which generate electricity by combustion, with an overall thermal rate having inputs of 50 megawatts and facilities for waste or recovery, other than spillways or hazardous waste with capacity of over 10 tonnes a day[cite: 13].
* **Group 2 priority**. Activities with facilities that generate electricity by combustion, with an overall thermal rate having inputs of under 50 megawatts or certain plants devoted to manufacturing metal processing with high productivity rates[cite: 13].
* **Group 3 priority**. Mining or waste management facilities with a low treatment rate[cite: 13].

To summarise, the companies in Appendix III, which are considered to be top priority, must, from 1 November 2018, have the financial guarantees under contract and filed with the competent body in their region that are required by the law to address potential environmental liability[cite: 13]. To this end, they must carry out a prior environmental risk assessment for their activity and, depending on the results of this, set up a financial guarantee to cover their environmental liability, which may be set at between 300,000 and 20 million euros, as a mandatory minimum, where this sum does not constitute a limit on the potential liability in a loss event situation[cite: 13]. The group 2 priority companies must have the same financial guarantee item, but this must be under contract and filed with the competent body in their regional administration before 1 November 2019[cite: 13]. Finally, we should say that there is still no legally regulated date by which the financial guarantees have to be obtained for the companies considered as having group 3 priority[cite: 13].

On the other hand, those companies are exempted from the financial guarantees which pursue activities that might cause damage for which the remedy is valued at a sum below 300,000 euros and those for which the remedy cost is between 300,000 and 2 million euros when they can accredit that the operator’s activities are certified by the EU’s Eco-Management and Audit Scheme (EMAS) or under the current UNE-EN-ISO 14001 standard[cite: 13].

Having said all this, there have been visibly significant changes and we must be optimistic, since what we have outlined leads us to conclude that there is a great opportunity for improvement, as well as scope for suggesting new proposals based on what has already been built for the benefit of all[cite: 13]. This is the right route in such an important topic as the one we are dealing with, which requires several factors such as those cited below[cite: 13]:

1. Sensitivity towards a matter that is fundamental for current sustainability and the development of future generations, making it necessary to establish a prior legislative strategy that allows the previous results to be obtained[cite: 13].
2. This is an arduous and difficult task to pull together and give cohesion to all the particular concepts of each Member State, offering a better alternative under a Community project aimed at the progress of society[cite: 13].
3. There is a fine-tuning process, certainly complicated to carry out, aimed at coupling concepts, ideas, or needs and giving a common final shape to the applicable legislation with the agreement of all participants[cite: 13].

From a legal standpoint, we Risk Managers erroneously called this matter Civil Environmental Liability[cite: 13]. However, this concept was not correct, since when environmental damage occurs, it can be fortuitous or caused by a liability of the corresponding company, thus being able to be defined as civil, criminal, or administrative environmental liability, depending on the type of offense committed[cite: 13]. As we already mentioned, it is, in this case, a regime in which the Public Administration acquires powers to guarantee compliance with the Law and the application of the liability scenarios it includes[cite: 13]. We reiterate, therefore, that this concept is separated from classic civil liability, in which conflicts between the party causing the damage and the injured party are dealt with in court[cite: 13].

## 5. The role of the Consorcio de Compensación de Seguros
The Law mentions that those operators who use the insurance option, among the possible choices as a mandatory financial guarantee, must supplement their policies with a contribution to the Environmental Damage Compensation Fund (FCDM), the management and administration of which is assigned to the Consorcio de Compensación de Seguros (CCS)[cite: 13].

This participation will consist of a specific surcharge on the insurance premium itself, which will then be transferred to the CCS by the insurers, the amount of which will be established through the corresponding rates approved by the Directorate-General for Insurance and Pension Funds[cite: 13]. We understand that the operating process is similar to the surcharge processed by insurers in collaboration with the CCS for Extraordinarily Risk coverage[cite: 13].

{{< callout >}}
The law establishes that “The Fund will be intended to extend insurance coverage for the liabilities insured in the original policy and under its same terms, for those damages that, having been caused by authorized activities during the validity period of the insurance, manifest or are claimed after the expiration of the manifestation or claim periods admitted in the policy, and are claimed within, at most, a number of years equal to that during which the insurance policy was in force, counted from when it ended and with a limit of 30 years.

However, given that the manifestation and claim periods admitted in the policy include the three years following the end of the insurance validity, the limit of coverage of the Consorcio de Compensación de Seguros would never exceed 27 years.

In the event that at any time the insurance was interrupted due to not having proceeded with its renewal, this interruption period will be excluded for the purposes of Fund coverage”.

“Among the modalities mentioned for establishing the aforementioned guarantee, operators may subscribe to an insurance policy, which must be complemented, in accordance with Article 33 of Law 26/2007 of 23 October, with a contribution to the Environmental Damage Compensation Fund (FCDM), the purpose of which is, compared to other guarantee modalities, to extend environmental risk coverage once the operator's activity ceases, extending to damages caused during the period of validity of the policy that manifest or are claimed later, up to a limit of 30 years from the end of the activity generating the damage. In this way, the policy of the ordinary insurer will cover the repair of environmental damage that manifests during the 3 years following the cessation of the operator's activity, while the Fund extends coverage from that moment and for a maximum of up to 27 additional years, at the rate of one year for each year that the operator has been covered by an insurance policy.

Environmental liability extends—and therefore FCDM coverage reaches, without prejudice to the particularities that may be contemplated in the legislation of each Autonomous Community—to the following environmental damages:

* Those caused to wild species, flora and fauna, and to habitats, terrestrial and aquatic areas.
* Those caused to surface or groundwater, in their ecological, chemical, or quantitative state, and damage to marine waters.
* Those caused to the sea coast and estuaries.
* Those affecting the soil and subsoil.

Damage to the air, persons, or property is not covered.

In any case, environmental liability is not enforceable against damage caused by: (i) armed conflicts, hostility, or insurrection; (ii) exceptional natural phenomena; and (iii) nuclear accidents.

For the purpose of disseminating general knowledge of the characteristics of the FCDM, these can be specified, among others, in the following points:

* The Consorcio de Compensación de Seguros acts, in accordance with the aforementioned Article 33 of Law 26/2007, as a mere administrator and manager of the FCDM, whose resources are financially and accounting-wise independent from the rest of its activities. In this way, the Consorcio will not have to bear any payment related to environmental damage charged to the assets of another of its activities.

* The FCDM is funded by contributions made by operators defined in the Law whose activities are capable of generating environmental damage, which will be collected by insurance companies along with the commercial premiums applied to their policyholders for mandatory financial guarantee coverage. In any case, the beneficiaries and recipients of FCDM resources are exclusively the operators.

* The contribution to the FCDM was set in the Resolution of the Directorate-General for Insurance and Pension Funds of 31 October 2018, at a rate of 8% of the commercial premium amount corresponding to the mandatory financial guarantee for environmental liability.

* The activity of the FCDM lacks an insurance nature in the strict sense, as the liability of the Fund is limited to the amount constituted within it.

* The mandatory financial guarantee of the FCDM is limited per event (environmental damage) to 20 million euros, with the possibility of a deductible being set by the Ministry for the Ecological Transition; an option that has not been contemplated to date.

* Compared to other guarantee modalities (bank guarantee and technical reserve), subscribing to an insurance policy means extending, through the FCDM, coverage for environmental damage repair beyond the damages manifested or claimed in the first 3 fiscal years after the interruption of ordinary policy coverage due to activity termination.

The validity of the Fund is defined in Order APM/1040/2017 of 23 October, which sets 31 October 2018 as the start date of its activity for priority 1 operators and one year later for priority 2 operators.”

On our part, it should be noted that, according to the articles of the law, this Fund does not grant coverage in the following cases[cite: 13]:

* Activities whose insurance has been canceled before ceasing activity[cite: 13].
* Damage generated after ceasing activity, due to abandonment of facilities with potential polluting capacity, without complying with mandatory measures to avoid said risk[cite: 13].
* Facts, damage, or liabilities that would not have had coverage under the insurance if the policy had been in force[cite: 13].
* Damage to air, persons, or property[cite: 13].
* Episodes of pollution conclusively discovered for the first time before three years have elapsed since the definitive cessation of the insured activity occurred[cite: 13]. For these purposes, the date of cessation of the insured activity is considered to be that on which mandatory operations for the remediation or decommissioning of facilities for future pollution prevention purposes concluded, or that on which the insured ceased performing any type of activity in the facility[cite: 13].
* Pollution cases claimed for the first time after the application period provided for in Article 4 of Law 26/2007 of 23 October has elapsed[cite: 13].
* Likewise, the Fund will not address coverage exclusions contemplated in the corresponding insurance policy, nor those established in Law 26/2007 of 23 October and its implementing provisions[cite: 13].

Similarly, insurance companies will have the obligation to retain information on insurance contracts subscribed for this purpose (financial guarantee), in order to provide the data required by the CCS at any given time, including activity, name of policyholder, operator, Tax Identification Number (NIF) of both, insured amounts, validity periods, and coverage conditions[cite: 13].

In the following link of the CCS, its Proposed Clause for inclusion in Insurance Policies with coverage from the Environmental Damage Compensation Fund (FCDM) can be reviewed: https://www.consorseguros.es/web/documents/10184/0/CLAUSULA_FCDM.pdf[cite: 13]

Finally, it should be highlighted that since 1 January 1998, the CCS has been integrated into the Spanish Environmental Risks Pool, an Economic Interest Grouping that began operations in 1994[cite: 13]. This Pool offers reinsurance coverage for damages caused by pollution that, in any case, must occur accidentally[cite: 13]. The participation of the CCS in the Pool since 2012 reaches a percentage of 5.9627%, which means a maximum retention of €1,140,000[cite: 13].

The Spanish Environmental Risks Pool offers specific insurance policies providing environmental liability solutions by: 1) restoring unexpected damage caused to natural resources; 2) covering legal defense expenses for guaranteed claims; 3) potentially providing judicial bonds; and 4) covering the cost of preventive measures in imminent risk situations of environmental damage[cite: 13]. Complementarily, within the same contract, civil liability coverage for third-party damage due to pollution is offered[cite: 13].

## 6. Risk management and environmental liability
We deliberately leave this section for the final part due to its obvious nature[cite: 13]. Dear reader, please review this article with patience once more and you will verify that everything mentioned relates to Risk Management[cite: 13].

Notice that to perform the Environmental Risk Assessment (ERA), each company must follow methodological steps such as the following[cite: 13]:

1. Identify and evaluate accidental scenarios and establish the probability of occurrence for each[cite: 13].

2. Quantify the severity of environmental damage consequences, including mitigation parameters generated in each scenario, to determine whether it is significant or not (for this, criteria set out in the Regulations of the Law are applied, indicating that factors to consider are intensity, extent, and duration of environmental damage)[cite: 13].

3. Estimate an Environmental Damage Index (IDM) associated with each accidental scenario following the methodology of the Ministry (software tool named MORA —Environmental Liability Supply Model)[cite: 13].

4. Calculate the “risk associated with each accidental scenario” as the product of scenario probability and the environmental damage index[cite: 13].

5. Calculate total facility risk by summing risks associated with each accidental scenario[cite: 13].

6. Select scenarios with lower associated environmental damage index grouping 95% of total risk[cite: 13].

7. Establish the financial guarantee amount as the environmental damage value of the scenario with the highest IDM among selected accidental scenarios, proceeding as follows[cite: 13]:

    * Quantify environmental damage generated in the selected scenario (highest IDM among those grouping 95% of total risk)[cite: 13].
    * Monetize environmental damage generated in said reference scenario, equal to the cost of the primary repair project[cite: 13]. For this, the Ministry's tool (MORA) is available[cite: 13]. Once the mandatory financial guarantee amount is determined, prevention and avoidance costs will be calculated, for which operators may apply a percentage to the total mandatory guarantee or estimate prevention/avoidance costs via environmental risk analysis[cite: 13].

8. Next, the company will contract the financial guarantee through options established in Chapter IV of the Law, mandatory for operations listed in Appendix III[cite: 13]. These aim to ensure the operator has sufficient economic resources to meet costs of adopting prevention, avoidance, and repair measures[cite: 13]. Article 24 assigns competent authorities the responsibility to establish financial guarantee amounts per activity type based on damage intensity and extent[cite: 13]. Amount determination must comply with economic valuation methodology for environmental damage repair approved by the National Government to ensure uniform nationwide application[cite: 13].

As indicated, three financial guarantee modalities are established, which may be set up alternatively or complementarily[cite: 13]:

    * Subscribing to an insurance policy with an authorized insurer in Spain, in which case the Consorcio de Compensación de Seguros assumes functions under Article 33[cite: 13].
    * Obtaining a bank guarantee granted by an authorized financial institution in Spain[cite: 13].
    * Setting up a technical reserve by funding an ad hoc reserve to respond to eventual environmental damage, materialized in public-sector backed financial investments[cite: 13].

9. The company will finally submit a Responsible Declaration to the Administration indicating financial guarantee establishment, amount, justification of operations performed under regulations, and legally required information[cite: 13].

10. It must also be remembered that Chapter V of the Law addresses the regime of offenses and penalties, an aspect not covered by the insurance policy if this modality is chosen[cite: 13]. Offenses categorized under Article 37 define non-compliant behaviors grouped into two categories: very serious and serious, based on harm to natural resources[cite: 13]. Fines range between €10,001 and €50,000 for serious offenses, and between €50,001 and €2,000,000 for very serious offenses[cite: 13]. In addition, temporary suspension of authorization may be imposed for up to two years (very serious) or one year (serious)[cite: 13].

For training purposes in Risk Management, it is important to reiterate the difference between environmental liability and liability for pollution[cite: 13].

Environmental liability refers exclusively to damage to natural resources (wild species and their habitat, aquifers, soil, and subsoil), which can only be claimed by Public Administrations against the holder of the economic or professional activity that caused said damage, as previously indicated[cite: 13]. In this case, the responsible party must minimize the damage, repair the consequences, and return the assets to their previous state, keeping the competent Administration informed[cite: 13].

On the other hand, in a loss involving damage caused by general civil liability (damage due to pollution), the damage is caused to property or persons and is claimed directly by the affected party[cite: 13]. In this case, the obligation will be to indemnify and repair the harm caused to the third party[cite: 13].

Various Forums and Seminars have highlighted this distinction (e.g., Inade Forum on 10 June 2018)[cite: 13].

We can conclude that, although traditionally action is taken after a loss occurs, in this case, we attempt to position ourselves proactively ahead of the loss in its management[cite: 13]. It is a regulation that does not permit direct monetary compensation to an injured party, but aims to prevent, evaluate, mitigate, repair, compensate, and return to pre-loss conditions, led by the Administration to set administrative standards and avoid judicial disputes, distinguishing civil liability from administrative liability while seeking sustainability and improvement for future generations[cite: 13]. Such an approach deserves our full support[cite: 13].

Furthermore, the social environment and its agents are collaborating: organizations and institutions such as the Ministry itself and the Spanish Environmental Risks Pool are carrying out extraordinary work in dissemination and awareness, creating insurance products that foster understanding and management of this issue[cite: 13].

Finally, in the realm of remediation and loss minimization, it is a source of pride to note how teams such as the Military Emergencies Unit (UME) or the Red Cross (among others) dedicate their efforts, resources, knowledge, skills, and courage to safeguard human lives—risking their own—protecting the environment and mitigating natural disasters through their actions[cite: 13].

Additionally, from a training standpoint, the annual plans of organizations such as the Spanish Association of Risk and Insurance Management (AGERS) include courses, seminars, presentations, and workshops on this topic due to its importance and the vital need to share knowledge, the foundation of education and a primary function of AGERS[cite: 13].

According to this legal compendium, establishing mandatory financial guarantees required by the latest 2017 Ministerial Order must respond to Environmental Risk Assessments (ERA), developing various tools such as UNE 150008:2008 (environmental risk analysis and evaluation), Model Environmental Risk Reports (MIRAT), Scale Tables, Methodological Guides, Environmental Damage Index (IDM), and the Environmental Liability Supply Model (MORA) to facilitate the required ERA and activate mandatory financial guarantees[cite: 13].
