## Introduction
In Greek mythology, the naiads (Naïαδες) were the spirits of small brooks, fountains, wells, springs, and other freshwater courses. Unlike the river gods, naiads were smaller and more adaptable to different forms. The NAIAD project, presented in this article, draws inspiration from this ancient river wisdom to tackle disasters. In particular, it focuses on the prevention and reduction phases of the disaster management cycle, viewing nature not only as part of the problem but also as part of the solution.

The ancient Greeks believed that all the waters of the world formed a single system, percolating from the sea into the earth through caverns and returning back to the sea. This systemic view of risk lies at the core of NAIAD. The approach also focuses on the versatility that nature brings and on understanding the protective role of nature-based solutions (NBS) in buffering risks generated by natural hazards through the development of natural assurance schemes.

Floods cause massive impacts worldwide. Numerous recent examples can be found across Europe (pluvial floods in Copenhagen, the Elbe floods of 2002 and 2013, the Danube floods in 2006, the Maritime Alps in 2015, the Lez River in 2014, the Seine in 2016 and 2018, etc.). Around 90% of natural risks are water-related and are likely to become more frequent and severe as a result of climate change. For instance, climate change is expected to increase damages in France by up to 50% by 2050 (Moncoulon et al. 2018). The Caisse Centrale de Réassurance (CCR) estimates that mean annual insured damages will grow by up to 38%, with 50% related to pluvial flooding and 34% to fluvial flooding respectively.

Climate change is already translating into higher risk levels from natural disasters and their associated damage costs (Lawrynuik 2019). Total reported economic losses from natural disasters in Europe over the 1980–2014 period reached 453 billion euros, of which only 45% was insured (EEA, 2019). Therefore, the (re)insurance sector is a critical actor to participate in, absorb, and evaluate the current and potential contributions of what we have termed Natural Assurance Schemes (NAS). The insurance sector has the opportunity to adopt different roles in loss prevention through NBS (Marchal et al. 2019). Catastrophe models developed by or for (re)insurers are tailored to evaluate the quantification of avoided damages via various preventive measures. This quantification can be complemented by estimating additional co-benefits identified by specialized institutions with expertise in the role of green infrastructure to deliver resilience dividends.

## 1. Main Objective and Strategic Goals
The main objective of the EU-funded NAIAD Project (1) is to develop a more comprehensive conceptual framework to better understand the insurable and assurance value of nature to facilitate natural risk prevention, incorporating nature into the equation not only as a problem but as a solution. NAIAD is funded by the European Commission under the Horizon 2020 program over three and a half years (2017–2020) with a budget of 5 million euros. This brief article presents the main results of developing this conceptual framework and operationalizing the ecosystem assurance value in disaster risk management, placing special emphasis on prevention and avoided damages thanks to nature investments.

The project is coordinated by the Duero River Basin Authority. It formed a consortium of 23 partners from 11 European countries, including 3 universities and research/educational institutes, 8 research centers, 4 small and medium enterprises, 3 public entities with competences in applied research, 2 public bodies with key management competencies (the City of Copenhagen and the Duero River Basin Authority), 2 NGOs, and the French public reinsurer CCR.

The project's objective aligns fully with the draft EU Adaptation Strategy “Adapting to Climate Change” (2) and the EU Taxonomy on Sustainable Finance document (3) (which includes 68 climate change adaptation activities published in March 2020), contributing to making our societies (and activities) more climate-resilient. NAIAD starts from the assumption that healthy and fully functioning ecosystems can significantly contribute to mitigating extreme hydrological risks and increasing societal resilience in the context of climate change.

{{< figura src="/images/numero-12/aseguranza01.png" caption="Figure 1: Location and main objectives of the NAIAD demos." >}}

To demonstrate the role ecosystems can play in reducing water-related risks (e.g., extreme and frequent events), the project developed what we term “Natural Assurance Schemes” (NAS). The project included 9 demonstration sites across Europe covering different scales: large scale in the Thames Basin (UK), Lower Danube (Romania), or Medina Aquifer (Spain); medium scale in the Lez, Brague (France), and Glinščica (Slovenia) catchments; and city scale in Copenhagen (Denmark), Rotterdam (Netherlands), and Lodz (Poland). These sites face diverse risks, predominantly focused on pluvial and fluvial flooding (Danube, Glinščica, Lez, and Brague), while the Medina Aquifer in Spain is oriented towards drought. Their sizes range from the smallest (a neighborhood in Rotterdam) to the largest (a 250 km stretch of the Lower Danube). They cover urban, peri-urban, and rural environments, using cities, basins, and aquifers as analytical units. Crucially, the demo sites range from completely new cases (such as Romania or Slovenia) to established sites where focus was placed on monitoring the efficiency of adopted natural flood management measures (such as the Lower Thames) rather than developing baseline evaluation frameworks.

With this, the project aims to propose new concepts and approaches to widen the portfolio of available solutions. Conventional infrastructure measures are costly: estimated required investment in water infrastructure over the next 15 years is 22 trillion dollars, representing more than half of the total expected infrastructure investment demand ($41 trillion) (WEF, 2019).

Three strategic goals were identified to achieve the central objective of demonstrating the ecosystem assurance value:

* Development of biophysical, social, and economic evaluation tools and methods to provide relevant decision-support and planning frameworks for identifying, co-designing, and stimulating NBS strategies in specific locations.
* Testing these tools and methods across the 9 real-environment demo sites, providing evidence on ecosystem value for disaster risk reduction (DRR) and ecosystem-based adaptation.
* Policy uptake and exploitation of project results, engaging decision-makers and legislators across various domains to validate results, identify next implementation steps, and pinpoint knowledge gaps requiring further R&D.

After briefly discussing the main conceptual framework, the following sections present the results corresponding to these three strategic goals.

## 2. Conceptual Framework: Natural Assurance Schemes for Adopting Nature-Based Solutions in Ecological DRR and Ecosystem-Based Adaptation
The importance of acting earlier in the risk management cycle to support climate change adaptation and Disaster Risk Reduction (DRR) has become increasingly clear. This involves adopting and standardizing NBS as an alternative or complement to conventional grey solutions to prevent or reduce risks, thereby increasing resilience and response capacity to hydrological hazards.

Interest in NBS has grown significantly in recent years. The European Commission (2015) defines nature-based solutions as those inspired and supported by nature, which are cost-effective, simultaneously provide environmental, social, and economic benefits, and build resilience. NAIAD focused particularly on how NBS can better prepare society and increase resilience against natural hazards, evaluating prevention value in terms of avoided damages and co-benefits: what we term “assurance value”. NBS fulfill multiple functions—from conserving or rehabilitating natural ecosystems to enhancing or developing natural processes in modified/artificial ecosystems—and can be applied at micro-scales (e.g., a small wetland) or macro-scales (e.g., floodplain restoration) (WWAP/UN-Water, 2018).

We established a conceptual distinction starting from the fact that insurance value can have several interpretations or components depending on the value dimension considered—specifically, what is being insured: the ecosystem or human assets? It also depends on the risk type considered. Hence the distinction between financial risk-transfer products (e.g., insuring a mangrove to protect its physical asset value) and the “assurance value of nature” (insuring the protective functions provided by nature itself, whether formally insured or not) (4). Theoretical literature on ecosystem insurance value exists (Pascual et al., 2010; Baümgartner & Strunz, 2014; Bartkovski et al., 2015), but practical operational methods enabling cities or basins to evaluate strategies incorporating NBS against grey alternatives or inaction remain scarce.

A suite of tools and methods was developed—and is briefly presented in this article (5)—designed to capture the natural assurance value in risk reduction provided by natural systems (Marchal et al. 2019) (6). This encompasses a comprehensive range of economic, financial, regulatory, institutional, and stakeholder-engagement mechanisms that regulate a sustainable and equitable flow of co-benefits between NBS and society. The resulting modular, structured approach incorporates (1) methodologies to assess and value NBS biophysically, socially, and economically, (2) risk perception concepts, and (3) implementation experiences across NAIAD case studies to operationalize all elements needed for environmental DRR enablement.

## 3. Biophysical, Social, and Economic Assessment Tools and Methods
The starting point for all demonstration projects was the biophysical assessment of exposure and risk. Several tools and methods were developed, tailored for application across different scales.

### 3.1. Biophysical Tools and Methods at Different Scales: Eco:Actuary and FEV Tools
The Eco:Actuary toolkit was developed for large-scale projects and comprises three main elements: First, the Eco:Actuary Decision Support System, a global catastrophe model mapping flood risk and exposure under present climate conditions and IPCC scenarios, as well as damage mitigation and avoided losses achieved via Natural Flood Management (NFM). Second, //Smart:River autonomous stations (see Figure 2), which utilize low-cost, DIY IoT-connected monitoring stations to track real-time river levels and quantify NFM contributions to flood risk reduction. Third, the Eco:Actuary Investment Planner, a simple spreadsheet-based online tool for evaluating the scale and approximate cost of interventions needed to reach a predefined flood reduction target. The insurance sector is particularly interested in catastrophe models as standard tools to assess risk severity and set appropriate insurance premiums. The Eco:Actuary tool allows users to alter scenarios to explore climate change impacts alongside mitigation options like land-use shifts, territorial management practices, or NFM interventions.

{{< figura src="/images/numero-12/aseguranza02.jpg" caption="Figure 2: Image of a //Smart:River autonomous station: using IoT for flood prevention." >}}

In several countries, national governments and the insurance industry closely align to boost flood risk reduction awareness. This demonstrates how insurance and risk mitigation interact: by investing in mitigation, insurance companies can significantly lower long-term claim payouts. //Smart uses low-cost IoT water-level logger networks to (a) evaluate mitigation measure effectiveness and (b) monitor extreme peak levels during flood events—serving either as early warning systems or as real-time data feeds for parametric insurance products (where payouts trigger automatically based on predefined thresholds like flood depth).

Methods were also developed for smaller scales, such as the Flood-Excess-Volume (FEV) method and tool, providing rapid cost-effectiveness assessments for flood mitigation strategies. To optimize generic flood mitigation strategies for specific catchments, an algorithmic protocol or "tool" is required that evaluates a wide range of measures in a clear, quantifiable, educational, and user-friendly manner accessible to non-experts. Developed through a collaboration between the University of Leeds (UK) and NAIAD, the tool was tested using real flood event data from the UK, France, and Slovenia (Bokhove et al., 2019, 2020) (see box below for details).

### 3.2. Participatory Modeling: Converting Obstacles into Opportunities for Collaboration and Collective Action
The second methodology focused on social assessment using a participatory modeling tool for co-designing NBS. This method manages risk perception ambiguity by fostering inclusive and balanced stakeholder engagement. Rooted in systems thinking, the model defines and analyzes complex, non-linear causal links influencing system behavior. The tool applies a sequential multi-phase process: i) eliciting and analyzing individual risk perceptions; ii) developing the systems thinking model; iii) detecting main barriers to NBS co-design and implementation; and iv) analyzing trade-offs and identifying conflicts. Phases i and iii allowed NAIAD to involve stakeholders and decision-makers in a co-design process focused on creating effective interventions to mitigate hydrological risks while delivering expected co-benefits. Phase iv methods enhanced equity in NBS implementation. Stakeholders recognized their contributions within the model, developing a sense of ownership over the results. Location-specific indicators were successfully developed and applied in the Glinščica catchment (Santoro et al., 2019; Pagano et al., 2019), Lower Danube (Giordano et al., 2020), and Medina Aquifer (Giordano et al., 2020).

The participatory modeling approach across NAIAD demo sites fostered inclusive, legitimate, and cooperative NAS design, boosting social acceptance of NBS. The modeling tool ensured inclusiveness and legitimacy by giving voice to diverse viewpoints regarding hydrological risk management, prioritizing rich knowledge collection over forced consensus. Cognitive mapping captured individual risk perceptions, local impacts, and social priorities. Integrating local stakeholder insights with scientific data yielded models perceived as legitimate, building strong stakeholder ownership.

Cooperation yielded two key advantages: First, stakeholder engagement built trust and relationships among participants, scientists, and decision-makers. Second, it unraveled complex interaction networks among decision-makers. Results demonstrated that dysfunctional interaction networks represent major barriers to NBS deployment. Consequently, targeted actions boosting institutional and non-institutional cooperation are essential for effective NBS implementation.

### 3.3. Integrated Cost-Benefit Analysis of Nature-Based Strategies
Economic evaluation is a core component of natural assurance scheme development. A detailed economic evaluation framework was created to compare primary costs and benefits generated by NBS for hydrological risks using monetary valuation methods:

* **Implementation Costs:** Direct expenses required to build and maintain NBS strategies.
* **Opportunity Costs:** Losses in economic yield from taking land out of production or reallocating it to NBS instead of profitable uses like commercial/residential development (indirect costs).
* **Avoided Damages:** Reduced economic losses resulting from hydrological risk mitigation via NBS (the primary direct benefit).
* **Co-benefits:** Additional environmental, economic, and social benefits generated by NBS.

Cost-Benefit Analysis (CBA) compares these costs and benefits over project lifespans across grey, hybrid, and NBS alternatives. Results show that NBS implementation costs are lower than grey infrastructure costs for equivalent risk reduction levels, reinforcing NBS cost-effectiveness arguments and encouraging systematic consideration by decision-makers. However, direct flood damage reduction benefits alone may not always fully cover initial capital and maintenance costs—posing challenges for single-sector risk mitigation financing. Comprehensive project designs tapping into all generated co-benefits are required.

Co-benefits (air quality improvement, urban heat island mitigation, landscape enhancement, carbon sequestration) represent the largest share of total value generated by NBS strategies. Integrating co-benefits is essential to make NBS projects economically advantageous. This multiplicity of benefits requires project developers to seek out and blend diverse funding and financing streams, supported by enabling policy frameworks.

To evaluate avoided damages, NAIAD leveraged CCR’s expertise using their Catastrophe (CAT) Model. The first unit models flood overflow and surface runoff hazards; the second geolocates insured property contracts at address-level with risk type and insured values; the third combines hazard intensity and exposure vulnerability via damage curves linking water stage/flow to structural damage rates. Ultimately, insured disaster losses are calculated in monetary terms (see CAT Model box).

Caisse Centrale de Réassurance (CCR), the French state reinsurer, collaborated with researchers from INRAE, BRGM, and the University of Nice within NAIAD to quantify NBS loss prevention roles and support local decision-making. CCR applied its CAT model to simulate flood events, evaluate hazards, and quantify avoided damages, incorporating climate change projections (Moncoulon et al., 2018).

CCR models direct tangible damages (floods, earthquakes, storm surges) under the French Natural Catastrophes Compensation Scheme. The CAT model combines hazard, vulnerability, and damage modules (Figure II). Overflow and runoff hazards are simulated via a 2D distributed model at 25m resolution calibrated nationally (Moncoulon et al., 2014).

The methodology was customized for French demo catchments and historic flood events by: 1. Collecting local land-use data; 2. Adjusting Digital Terrain Model (DTM) slope calculations to track grid-level water depth along short watercourses.

Flood hazard simulations were validated against historical flood marks and spatial extents. Insured claims data for selected events were extracted at address resolution. Damage rate curves linking water flow/depth to destruction rates (DR = claims paid / insured value) were calibrated for residential, commercial, industrial, and agricultural assets: $TD_r = f(\text{m}^3/\text{s} \text{ or } \text{m})$.

Simulated losses were validated against actual historic claims. Hazard intensity reduction scenarios (10–50% reduction ranges) were then applied to model avoided damages. For example, in the Lez catchment during a 2014-scale flood event, a 50% runoff hazard reduction saves residential property owners ~€1.9M (a 40.45% damage reduction).

Integrating stochastic 400-year rainfall simulations (ARPEGE-Climat) under present and 2050 climate conditions (Moncoulon et al., 2018) allows modeling future average annual losses. Results indicate that in the Lez catchment, NBS must achieve a 35% hazard reduction to offset projected climate change impacts and maintain annual average losses at current levels.

## 4. Knowledge Integration: Adaptive Planning, New Business Models, and Innovative Sustainable Financing
Multidisciplinary knowledge from biophysical, social, and economic analyses across demo sites was synthesized into a global decision-support framework applicable to varying technical, environmental, and socioeconomic contexts.

Complementary tools were developed to integrate evaluation elements, such as a stakeholder engagement protocol guiding problem identification, solution selection, and strategy co-design (Annex 1). This process connected directly into an adaptive planning cycle and a dedicated Natural Assurance Business Canvas to identify revenue streams, blended finance options, impact investments, and performance-based contracts.

### 4.1. The Adaptive Planning Cycle
Grounded in adaptive planning frameworks (Basco-Carrera et al., 2020; van Cauwenbergh et al., 2020), adaptive planning is a structured, iterative decision-making process for managing long-term uncertainty through continuous monitoring and learning. Using study-specific models, Dynamic Adaptive Policy Pathways (DAPP) can be developed to establish flexible long-term strategic plans that commit to short-term actions while defining clear frameworks to adjust future actions as conditions evolve.

### 4.2. The Natural Assurance Business Model Canvas
The Natural Assurance Business Model Canvas (Mayor et al., 2020) adapts traditional conceptual business tools (Osterwalder & Pigneur, 2010) to define value propositions, delivery mechanisms, and financial structures for natural assurance schemes. The NAS Canvas differs in two key ways: First, it structures logic around ecosystem service supply and demand; second, it incorporates pluralistic valuation (Sanders et al., 2016) and relational values (Muraca & Himes, 2018) within the IPBES framework (7)—defined as desirable relationships between nature and people (Díaz et al., 2015). The canvas captures public, collective, and private values, facilitating multi-stakeholder alignment and willingness-to-pay for multifunctional NBS strategies delivering concurrent collective benefits over time (Jiang et al., 2016).

{{< figura src="/images/numero-12/aseguranza06.png" caption="Figure 4: Example of NAS Business Canvas applied to the Lower Danube." >}}

### 4.3. Financial Framework for Water Security
The financial framework for water security (Altamirano et al., 2020) adapts the "Five Case Model" (Smith & Flanagan, 2001) for natural assurance schemes across five analytical dimensions:
a) **Strategic Case:** Demonstrates strategic alignment and a compelling case for change.
b) **Economic Case:** Evaluates investment options (green, hybrid, grey) to identify the most cost-effective solution.
c) **Commercial Case:** Ensures commercial viability and deal structure feasibility for NBS.
d) **Financial Case:** Confirms financial affordability and funding availability.
e) **Management Case:** Verifies delivery capabilities and governance processes, typically led by public authorities directly or indirectly exposed to natural hazards.

NBS face specific adoption barriers, including limited access to capital due to a lack of operational real-world track records needed to build investor confidence and de-risk capital. Making NBS attractive to private and impact investors requires clear value proposition quantification and robust business cases ensuring medium-to-long-term returns. Identifying a project's "business model"—quantifying value propositions, operational assets/partners, delivery costs, beneficiaries, revenue streams, and blended financing packages covering lifecycle capital, operational, and opportunity costs—is essential for de-risking investor perceptions.

## 5. Case Study Results: Implementation and Lessons Learned
This section highlights key findings from selected large- and small-scale NAIAD case studies: Spangen (Rotterdam) and its replication in Valladolid (Spain), the Lower Danube, the Thames Basin, and Medina del Campo.

### 5.1. Co-Design Process, Problem Ownership, and Legitimacy: Lower Danube (Romania)
Covering a 250 km stretch of the Danube River, the Lower Danube demo focused on floodplain restoration to reduce regional flood and drought risks. Guided by the stakeholder engagement protocol (Annex 1), local/regional authorities, insurers, farmers, and basin managers co-designed risk mitigation strategies. Stakeholders identified NBS—specifically restoring the Potelu Lagoon and Bireț floodplains—as the preferred option. Direct benefits (flood risk reduction and avoided damages) were quantified using CCR’s CAT model.

Indirect co-benefits included local economic development, biodiversity enhancement, soil erosion control, and reduced rural-to-urban migration. Through structured co-design, stakeholders mapped problem ownership, implementation responsibilities, regulatory frameworks, direct/indirect beneficiaries (via the NAS Canvas), and capital/O&M cost estimates. Key operational requirements identified included data sharing, governance adaptation, and land-use regulatory revisions. Monitoring indicators were established to track long-term risk reduction and co-benefit delivery.

### 5.2. NBS Effectiveness: Comparing Flood and Drought Options in the Lower Thames (UK)
The Eco:Actuary tool was co-developed with the insurance industry and applied in the Thames Basin alongside local authorities and landowners. NAIAD facilitated engagements with green finance investors exploring Natural Flood Management (NFM) and drought-resilient agricultural practices (e.g., regenerative agriculture), using //Smart: systems to quantify risk reduction. Actuarial associations explored developing a global climate risk index based on Eco:Actuary. Additionally, a UK water utility invested in NAIAD //Smart: technology to guide farmers on soil tillage and cover crop techniques to reduce nitrate leaching and soil erosion across drought-prone chalk aquifers.

Evaluation results confirmed that investing in regenerative agriculture represents the most cost-effective strategy for drought mitigation. Increasing soil infiltration retains water on-site, effectively mitigating downstream flood risks while maintaining water availability during dry seasons. Regenerative farming delivers multiple co-benefits: reduced tillage carbon emissions, enhanced soil organic matter, increased biodiversity, and reduced agricultural runoff pollution.

### 5.3. Policy Contextualization: Policy Guidelines for Water Security in Medina del Campo (Spain)
The Medina del Campo study addressed overexploited aquifer risks under the Groundwater Directive (2006/118/EC) and Water Framework Directive (2000/60/EC), which mandate the Duero River Basin Authority (CHD) to achieve good groundwater status by 2027. Primary pressures include declining water tables, diffuse agricultural nitrate ($NO_3$) pollution, and natural arsenic contamination. CHD's initial intervention involved surface water transfers from the Adaja River and Cogotas Reservoir to replace groundwater abstraction across 6,000 hectares of irrigated farmland, successfully driving local piezometric recovery by halting abstractions and increasing surface irrigation recharge.

NAIAD supported CHD in identifying and evaluating NAS strategies combining NBS and soft measures to mitigate risks and restore aquifer functionality. Following the stakeholder engagement protocol (Annex 1), CHD assessed technical and legal feasibility for two drought-resilient NAS strategies:

1. **Crop Switching & Conservation:** Promoting drought-resistant crop varieties, soil/water conservation practices, water user associations, abstraction monitoring, and environmental awareness.
2. **Managed Aquifer Recharge (MAR):** Combining artificial recharge infrastructure, water user associations, abstraction control, and public education.

Groundwater flow was simulated under three climate scenarios and three management regimes: 1) Business-As-Usual (BAU); 2) Exploitation Index (EI) reduction to 0.85 by 2050; and 3) EI reduction to 0.80 by 2050 (CHD target). Results demonstrated that groundwater management (EI reduction) exerts a significantly greater impact on piezometric recovery than climate-driven recharge changes.

Economic evaluations of both strategies were conducted using Le Coent et al. (2020) CBA frameworks. Socioeconomic and regulatory scenarios incorporated stakeholder-identified drivers, notably European Common Agricultural Policy (CAP) subsidy evolutions (comparing current CAP against eco-directed CAP scenarios). Climate projections (IPCC RCP 4.5 and RCP 8.5) showed no statistically significant mean annual rainfall trends for 2050 (CEDEX, 2012; Llorente et al., 2018), leading to baseline historic trend simulations (Calatrava et al., 2019).

Economic impacts were modeled using a calibrated agro-economic tool simulating land and water allocation across crop alternatives. Non-monetized co-benefits were evaluated qualitatively and quantitatively, indicating gains in water productivity, agricultural employment, and farm income. Environmentally, both strategies improved aquifer quantitative/qualitative status, though Strategy 2 (MAR) accelerated recovery and benefited riverine ecosystems, while Strategy 1 promoted lower-intensity farming through crop rotation.

Economic evaluations confirmed that Strategy 1 benefits far exceeded costs (Benefit-Cost Ratio = 3.17), even excluding indirect co-benefits (Calatrava et al., 2019). Strategy 2 enhanced local riverine ecosystems without directly reducing agricultural drought risk. However, Strategies 1 and 2 are highly complementary; combining them accelerates aquifer recovery while maximizing environmental co-benefits, underscoring the necessity of integrated valuation frameworks (López-Gunn et al., 2020).

### 5.4. Replication: Climate-Resilient Stadiums via NBS—Rotterdam Flood Mitigation and Valladolid Drought Resilience
NAIAD's smallest demo site evaluated neighborhood flood mitigation in Spangen (Rotterdam), where an NBS was installed to increase stormwater retention and relieve sewer overflow pressures during extreme rainfall events. The neighborhood suffered from insufficient retention capacity and drought-related vulnerabilities (urban heat stress, wooden pile foundation degradation). Rotterdam City Council identified a required retention deficit of $53,000\text{ m}^3$.

To address this, the city implemented an "Urban Water Buffer" (UWB) pilot around Sparta Rotterdam’s football stadium. The UWB captures, treats, and stores rainwater runoff from the stadium roof and surrounding streets in subsurface aquifers for pitch irrigation. The system adds $1,500\text{ m}^3$ of retention capacity every 48 hours, simultaneously mitigating flood and drought risks.

NAIAD conducted an economic evaluation comparing three neighborhood-wide flood mitigation strategies:
* **Grey:** Separate sewer systems and permeable paving.
* **Hybrid:** Separate sewers combined with natural retention, public square infiltration, and aquifer storage.
* **Green:** Exclusive reliance on green retention and infiltration infrastructure.

The CBA evaluated direct flood mitigation alongside indirect co-benefits (health impacts, property value appreciation, heat island mitigation, green roof lifespan extension, and potable water savings via reuse).

Results demonstrated that grey solution implementation costs were 15% higher than green NBS for equivalent risk reduction, confirming NBS cost-effectiveness. Furthermore, estimated green co-benefits far exceeded direct flood mitigation value, presenting a compelling investment case.

This solution is currently being replicated at the José Zorrilla football stadium in Valladolid (Spain), combining resources with the Horizon 2020 UrbanGreenUp project, funding from the Dutch *Partners for Water* innovation fund and Valladolid City Council, and technical support from local water utility Aquavall, Real Valladolid C.F., and the Duero River Basin Authority. This demonstrates how multi-stakeholder partnerships between municipalities, sports clubs, water utilities, basin agencies, European startups, and research institutions can deploy NBS to mitigate flood and drought risks while generating substantial co-benefits.

## 6. Policy Uptake and Exploitation
NAIAD demonstrated the potential of NBS and green infrastructure as future-proof investments for climate adaptation and flood/drought resilience across urban and catchment scales. Resilience gains are built upon avoided damages and natural co-benefits. By introducing Natural Assurance Schemes (NAS), cities and regions can leverage standardized tools: Eco:Actuary and FEV for biophysical assessment, participatory modeling for stakeholder risk perception, integrated CBA, adaptive planning pathways, the NAS Business Model Canvas, and water security financial frameworks.

During the final demo meeting in Copenhagen (January 2020), a simulated investor pitch brought together real public and private investors to identify requirements for converting technical knowledge into bankable NAS projects.

Policy roundtables were conducted across European countries (Spain, France, Romania, Sweden, Slovakia) to validate findings and align results with major European policy initiatives, including the EU Climate Adaptation Strategy, the European Climate Law, and the EU Sustainable Finance Taxonomy. Discussions with the European Commission (DG CLIMA) focused on deploying NBS and NAS to address three critical policy gaps: the protection gap, the investment gap, and the information-sharing gap.

NAIAD provided an integrated conceptual framework and toolkit to quantify ecosystem assurance values across 9 European demo sites spanning diverse scales and implementation stages. These frameworks support embedding NAS into river basin management and restoration planning. Project outputs include open-access training materials for an online MOOC course and an upcoming academic volume published by Springer.

## 7. Conclusions

After more than three years of research, NAIAD provides definitive answers to core disaster management questions:

First, in the context of climate change, land-use shifts, and expanding asset exposure, disaster damages are escalating rapidly—challenging governments, local authorities, insurers, and citizens. Are NBS effective solutions? Can natural assurance schemes improve preparedness while reducing potential costs? Based on project evidence, the answer is unequivocally affirmative.

Second, NAIAD demonstrates that NBS represent vital components of risk reduction portfolios, enhancing systemic resilience while delivering societal co-benefits. However, NBS are not silver bullets; combining NBS with grey infrastructure in hybrid configurations often represents the optimal choice. Developing tailored local solutions requires real-world testing and empirical review. Multidisciplinary integration is essential to address complex hydrological challenges. Simulations indicate that NBS are exceptionally effective at mitigating frequent, lower-magnitude events, thereby elevating baseline system resilience and maximizing prevention-phase assurance values.

Third, standardized NAS evaluation tools facilitate embedding green solutions into river basin management, flood/drought risk planning, and capital allocation—shifting the risk management paradigm toward proactive prevention.

Finally, a major lesson learned involves identifying and resolving stakeholder barriers, particularly surrounding risk perception ambiguity. Mobilizing collective action requires unpacking "transaction costs" to unlock collective risk reduction powered by the inherent protective value of nature.

## Annex 1. Stakeholder Engagement Protocol: Co-Designing Natural Assurance Schemes
In parallel with technical analyses, multiple local stakeholder workshops were conducted across demo sites. NAIAD partners co-developed identical implementation steps alongside local stakeholders to support effective NBS deployment.

Workshop deliverables included:
* Local risk perception evaluation.
* Selection and validation of optimal solutions (including grey and hybrid measures).
* Identification of associated co-benefits.
* Co-definition of long-term monitoring indicators to evaluate risk reduction performance and co-benefit delivery.
* Detailed analysis of NBS strategies and optimal multi-measure combinations.
* Final workshop co-identification of potential business models for chosen solutions.

### Box 1: NAIAD Stakeholder Engagement Protocol

A core operational principle of NAIAD is proactive engagement with local stakeholders across study cases throughout conceptual and evaluative methodology deployments.
